Designing for the second look

By Hana Whitfield · 9 min read

The first glance gets all the attention. It is where the hero image lives and where the headline fights for its life. But most of what makes a site feel considered happens later, in the second look, when someone has stayed long enough to notice the details.

That is where spacing, rhythm and typography do their quiet work. Nobody comments on a well-set paragraph; they simply keep reading. The best interfaces are rarely praised, only used, again and again, without friction.

Start with the reading experience

Before choosing colours or components, set a single paragraph of real content in your chosen typeface. Adjust the size until it is comfortable, the line height until it breathes, and the measure until your eyes never get lost on the way back to the next line.

If it works for a paragraph, it will work for a page. If it does not, no amount of decoration will save it. Everything else is a layer on top of that foundation.

Good design is what is left after you take away everything that was only there to be noticed.

Make progress visible

Long pages benefit from small signals of orientation. A thin progress line, a sticky title, an estimate of time remaining: none of them are essential, yet together they tell the reader where they are and how much is left, which makes it easier to commit to the next section.

Keep these signals understated. They should feel like the margin of a book, present when you look for them and invisible when you do not.

Leave room at the end

The end of an article is a moment of goodwill. Resist the urge to fill it with banners. A single, clear next step, a related piece or a simple thank you is almost always enough.

When the reader reaches the bottom, the line is full, the percentage reads one hundred, and the page has quietly told them that they finished something worth finishing.

A final note on restraint

Every effect on a page competes with the content for attention. Choose the ones that serve the reader, tune them until they are barely there, and let the writing do the talking.
